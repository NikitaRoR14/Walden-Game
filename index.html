Ralph Waldo Emerson

1803 - 1882

"To be yourself in a world that is constantly trying to make you something else is the greatest accomplishment"

The Sage of Concord

Ralph Waldo Emerson was America's greatest philosopher, essayist, and poet of the 19th century. He founded the Transcendentalist movement, which emphasized the inherent goodness of people and nature, and the corruption of society and institutions.

Thoreau's Mentor

Emerson was Thoreau's mentor, friend, and benefactor. He owned the land where Thoreau built his cabin at Walden Pond and encouraged his experiment. Though they sometimes disagreed on philosophy and methods, their friendship shaped American literature and thought.

Emerson famously said of Thoreau after his death: "The country knows not yet, or in the least part, how great a son it has lost."

Major Works

  • Nature (1836) - Founding document of Transcendentalism
  • Self-Reliance (1841) - His most famous essay on individualism
  • The American Scholar (1837) - Called "America's intellectual Declaration of Independence"
  • Essays: First & Second Series - Including "Experience," "Circles," "The Poet"

Philosophy

Emerson believed in the divinity of nature, the power of individual intuition over tradition, and the importance of self-reliance. He taught that every individual contains divine spark and should trust their inner voice over societal convention.

His ideas influenced not just Thoreau, but Walt Whitman, Emily Dickinson, and countless others who sought to live authentic, examined lives.

"What lies behind us and what lies before us are tiny matters compared to what lies within us."
— Ralph Waldo Emerson

Thoreau's Cabin at Walden

Built 1845

"I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately"

The Experiment

On July 4, 1845, Henry David Thoreau moved into a small cabin he had built near Walden Pond in Concord, Massachusetts. He lived there for two years, two months, and two days, seeking to "live deliberately" and discover the essential facts of life.

By the Numbers

  • Cost to Build: $28.12½ (about $1,000 today)
  • Size: 10 feet by 15 feet (150 square feet)
  • Features: One room, fireplace, bed, table, desk, three chairs
  • Distance to Town: 1.5 miles from Concord center
  • Land: Owned by Ralph Waldo Emerson

Daily Life

Thoreau's days began before dawn. He bathed in the pond, watched the sunrise, and spent mornings writing in his journal. He worked in his bean field for income, walked four hours daily observing nature, read classics by candlelight, and received occasional visitors.

Contrary to myth, he wasn't a hermit—he walked to town frequently, dined with family and friends, and welcomed visitors to his cabin.

The Three Chairs

Thoreau wrote: "I had three chairs in my house; one for solitude, two for friendship, three for society." This simple statement captured his philosophy of balance between being alone, intimate connection, and community.

Why He Left

Thoreau left Walden on September 6, 1847, not because he failed, but because he succeeded. He wrote: "I left the woods for as good a reason as I went there. Perhaps it seemed to me that I had several more lives to live, and could not spare any more time for that one."

His experiment proved that one could live richly on very little, that simplicity brought clarity, and that deliberate living was possible in a rapidly industrializing America.

Legacy

The original cabin no longer exists (Thoreau dismantled it in 1849), but a replica stands near the pond today. The site is a pilgrimage destination for readers of Walden, seekers of simplicity, and those questioning modern life's assumptions.

"I learned this, at least, by my experiment: that if one advances confidently in the direction of his dreams, and endeavors to live the life which he has imagined, he will meet with a success unexpected in common hours."
— Henry David Thoreau, Walden

Henry David Thoreau

1817 - 1862

"I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately"

Early Life

Born David Henry Thoreau in Concord, Massachusetts, he later reversed his name to Henry David. He graduated from Harvard College in 1837, where he studied rhetoric, classics, philosophy, mathematics, and science. Despite his education, he rejected conventional career paths, choosing instead to become a philosopher, naturalist, and writer.

Walden Pond Experiment (1845-1847)

At age 28, Thoreau built a small cabin on land owned by his mentor Ralph Waldo Emerson near Walden Pond. For two years, two months, and two days, he lived simply, deliberately, and self-sufficiently. He grew beans, observed nature, read classics, wrote in his journal daily, and questioned the assumptions of American life.

His cabin cost $28.12½ to build—a fraction of what his neighbors spent on houses. This wasn't mere frugality; it was a philosophical experiment about the relationship between cost and life itself.

Civil Disobedience

In July 1846, Thoreau was arrested for refusing to pay his poll tax in protest of slavery and the Mexican-American War. He spent one night in jail (his aunt paid the tax). This experience became "Civil Disobedience" (1849), one of history's most influential essays on nonviolent resistance.

His principle was simple but radical: an unjust law is no law at all, and a just person's place under an unjust government is in prison.

Major Works

  • Walden (1854) - His masterpiece, detailing his experiment in simple living and containing profound insights on economy, reading, solitude, and nature
  • Civil Disobedience (1849) - Essay on individual resistance to unjust government
  • Walking (1862) - Contains his famous line: "In wildness is the preservation of the world"
  • His Journals - Over 2 million words documenting 24 years of observation and thought

Philosophy & Legacy

Core Beliefs:

  • Live deliberately and resist mindless conformity
  • Simplify material life to enrich spiritual life
  • The cost of a thing is the life exchanged for it
  • In wildness is the preservation of the world
  • Individual conscience over unjust law
  • Self-education and direct experience over formal schooling

Historical Impact:

  • Mahatma Gandhi adopted his civil disobedience philosophy for India's independence
  • Martin Luther King Jr. used his principles in the Civil Rights Movement
  • John Muir was inspired by his nature writing to create National Parks
  • Environmental Movement - Father of modern conservation and ecology
  • Minimalism & Voluntary Simplicity - Pioneered anti-consumerist philosophy
  • Counterculture Movements - Inspired 1960s back-to-the-land movement

Later Life & Death

After Walden, Thoreau returned to Concord, living with his family while working as a surveyor, lecturer, and writer. He became increasingly radical in his opposition to slavery, helping fugitive slaves escape via the Underground Railroad and defending John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry.

He died of tuberculosis at age 44 on May 6, 1862. His last words were reportedly "moose" and "Indian"—fitting for a man who loved wilderness. At his funeral, his friend Emerson said: "The country knows not yet, or in the least part, how great a son it has lost."

— Henry David Thoreau, Walden
